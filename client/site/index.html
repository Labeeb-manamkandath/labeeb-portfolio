AI Voice Insight Generator

The Real Problem

Nobody had time to write reports.

Construction sites run on speed. A field engineer managing 40+ workers doesn't pause work to fill a digital form. So they don't. Updates go to WhatsApp. Issues get buried. Management guesses at project health by calling site supervisors at end of day.

This wasn't a user motivation problem. It was a tool design problem — the existing reporting system demanded behaviour change, not tool change. I needed to flip that.

"The form takes 10 minutes. I don't have 10 minutes. I have 10 seconds between tasks."

Site engineer, user interview · Dubai construction project
3
Root causes identified

Forms were too structured, required switching context, and had no language flexibility for multilingual crews.

5
Stakeholders mapped

Field engineers, site supervisors, project managers, operations heads, and the CEO — each with a different need from the same data.

12
User interviews conducted

Across 4 active construction sites. Behaviour observed in context — not just self-reported in a meeting room.

Discovery & Research

What I actually found in the field.

Voice is the natural medium for fieldwork

Workers already narrated issues verbally to supervisors. The mental model existed — we just needed to capture it digitally without friction.

Language was a silent barrier

65% of crew members weren't comfortable reporting in English. This meant details got lost in translation or not reported at all. Language support wasn't a nice-to-have — it was the adoption blocker.

Managers needed a briefing, not a feed

Project managers checked updates before morning meetings. They needed a pre-digested "top 10" view — not 200 individual field entries to scroll through. Volume was noise.

Visual evidence made updates credible

When field workers included a photo, management acted faster. Without it, reports were often questioned. Trust lived in visual proof.

AI classification needed to be trustworthy

Early testing showed users distrust AI labels when they feel arbitrary. I needed the classification logic to feel obvious — not magic. The colour coding (green/red) had to match mental models, not just data logic.

User Flows & Information Architecture

Three principles that drove every decision.

Principle 01
Remove structure from the input

If users had to think about how to format their report, they wouldn't report. The mic button was the entire entry point. No fields. No categories to pick. Just speak. AI handles the rest.

Principle 02
Add structure at the output

Management needed to see structure — by project, by date, by severity. The AI classification layer did this transformation invisibly. Field worker speaks freely. Manager sees organised data.

Principle 03
Earn trust through transparency

Every AI-classified entry showed the user what the AI understood. Users could correct it. This gave them ownership over the output and increased trust in the system over time.

Design Journey

How my thinking evolved through iterations.

These were the pivotal design decisions — not just what changed, but why the earlier version was wrong and what the research told me.

Decision
Before
After
Recording Entry Point

A standard form with a "voice note" field embedded alongside text inputs. Mic was one option among many.

The mic became the entire screen. One large, impossible-to-miss button. No competing elements. Research showed that cognitive load at the entry point was the #1 reason workers abandoned reports mid-way.

Language Selection

Language auto-detected from device settings. One less step for the user.

Explicit language picker before recording. Auto-detect failed on multilingual devices and created wrong transcriptions. Workers lost trust in the output. Manual selection took 2 seconds and dramatically improved accuracy and confidence.

AI Classification Display

AI silently tags entries as Issue or Progress in the background. Clean, invisible experience.

After recording, the user sees the AI's classification with an option to correct it. Usability testing revealed that "invisible AI" felt untrustworthy — users wanted to verify the system understood them. Surfacing the classification and making it editable increased trust scores by 60% in testing.

Photo Annotation

Photos attached as raw images. No editing tools — keep it lightweight.

Simple annotation layer (circle, highlight, arrow) added after photo capture. Field test showed that managers couldn't always understand what in a photo was the issue. Adding annotations reduced back-and-forth clarification calls by approximately 35% in pilot testing.

Management Dashboard

Chronological feed of all voice insights, filterable by date and project.

"Top 10 Insights" section pinned at the top, with the full feed below, AI-curated. Managers in testing said they had no time to read 100 entries — they wanted the critical ones surfaced automatically. Top 10 became the most-used dashboard element in the first week of launch.

Challenges I Solved

The hard design problems.

How do you design for users who distrust AI?
Problem

Testing showed workers felt AI was "guessing" their intent. When it got the classification wrong, they stopped trusting the whole system — not just that one entry.

Solution

Surfaced the AI's interpretation immediately after recording. Added a one-tap correction mechanism. This shifted perception from "AI decides" to "AI suggests, I confirm" — and adoption jumped.

Multilingual voice input in noisy construction environments
Problem

Background noise, mixed-language sentences, and heavy accents caused transcription errors. Auto-detected language often failed for bilingual speakers.

Solution

Explicit language selection pre-recording. Added a "re-record" option if the transcription looked wrong. Designed the verification screen so users could scan the output quickly before submitting.

Designing a management view that isn't just a feed
Problem

Early dashboard prototypes showed everything chronologically. In testing, managers opened it, saw 80 entries, and closed it. Information overload killed the value proposition entirely.

Solution

Introduced AI curation — the "Top 10 Insights" logic. This required close collaboration with the AI team to define what "important" meant. We weighted by Issue classification, recency, and project priority.

Getting 40+ year old site supervisors to use a new digital tool
Problem

Target users included experienced site veterans who were resistant to digital tools and had never used voice-based apps. Onboarding friction was a real adoption risk.

Solution

Designed the entry flow to mirror how they already behaved — talking. No new mental model required. First-time use was 3 taps. No tutorial screen needed. "I just tapped the big button and it worked" — direct feedback from a 52-year-old site supervisor in pilot testing.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

AI should feel like a co-worker, not a black box

The biggest design lesson: making AI decisions visible and editable wasn't about doubting the AI — it was about making users feel in control. Trust isn't given, it's designed.

Language is a design problem, not a dev problem

Treating multilingual support as a technical feature missed the point. It was a UX decision that shaped the entire IA and flow — the language picker had to be before the mic, not buried in settings.

Less data, more signal

The management dashboard taught me that showing everything is the same as showing nothing. Information design is as much about what you remove as what you show.

The Real Problem

Nobody knew who was actually in the vehicle.

On the surface, the ask was simple: "show routes in an app." But the real problem ran deeper. Drivers didn't know who was actually in the vehicle, when the next job started, or how long they had before they needed to leave. Dispatchers were equally blind.

The result was a constant loop of phone calls, WhatsApp messages, missed pickups, and idle time. Drivers were managing routes across three different channels simultaneously — while driving. That's not a productivity problem. That's a safety problem.

"The deeper problem was uncertainty and friction — drivers didn't know who was actually in the vehicle, when the next job starts, or how long until they must leave."

Problem framing from field observation sessions
3
Channels drivers monitored simultaneously

Phone calls, WhatsApp groups, and a basic job list app — none of them showing a shared, real-time view of who's in the car.

2
Core user types with conflicting needs

Drivers needed glanceable, one-tap simplicity. Dispatchers needed accurate status visibility and route verification. One product had to serve both.

4
Critical friction points identified

Missed pickups, wrong staff check-ins, no buffer time visibility, and no way to undo a mistake without calling dispatch.

User Flows & Information Architecture

Three principles for an interface used while driving.

Principle 01
Glanceability

Show the whole day in one view. A driver pulling up to a stop needs to understand their next three hours in under 2 seconds — before they open the car door.

Principle 02
Low friction

Large touch targets and one-tap actions only. No multi-step confirmations, no navigation to a second screen to complete a core task. If it takes more than one tap, it's too slow.

Principle 03
Recoverability

Mistakes happen on the road. Every action — check-in, drop-off, pick-up — must be undoable in one tap without calling dispatch, filling a form, or losing any data.

Visual Language

A colour system drivers read without thinking.

Every screen element had to be interpretable at a glance. I built a four-state badge system that drivers learn in the first 60 seconds and never have to re-learn.

Checked-in — staff is in the vehicle
Pending — not yet confirmed
Not in car — absent or unassigned
Declined — staff rejected the job
Design Journey

The pivotal decisions that shaped the final product.

These weren't just visual choices — each one came from a real finding in field observation or usability testing that forced a rethink.

Decision
Before

Information Architecture

A list view showing jobs sorted by time, similar to a calendar app. Clean, familiar, standard.

A timeline-centred view where each job is a large card with location map, time window, staff list, and two primary CTA buttons visible without scrolling. Field testing showed that list views forced drivers to mentally reconstruct the day — the timeline made the whole day instantly readable as a sequence.

Staff Visibility

Staff status shown only inside individual job cards — tap the job to see who's in the vehicle.

A persistent "Staffs in my car (N)" button always visible on the main timeline, opening a roster modal with Check-in, Undo, Drop-off, and Pick-up actions. Research showed the most common driver anxiety was not knowing their current passenger count — making it persistent removed that anxiety entirely.

Check-in Action

Inline check-in within the job card, small tap target, adjacent to other buttons.

Large full-width green Check-in button as the primary CTA. Red Undo button immediately accessible. Button size was driven by the use context — drivers are often in motion, glancing at the screen for under 3 seconds. Small targets caused mis-taps that required calling dispatch to correct.

Time Buffer Visibility

No time buffer display — drivers could see job start times but had to mentally calculate travel time themselves.

Live countdown between jobs: "30 mins to start next job." This single element reduced late departures significantly in testing — drivers stopped relying on memory and gut instinct for departure timing and trusted the visible countdown instead.

Mistake Recovery

Wrong check-ins required contacting dispatch to manually correct — treated as an exception, not part of the main flow.

One-tap Undo built into every action as a first-class feature, not an afterthought. Drivers in testing described the Undo button as "the most important thing in the app" — knowing they could correct a mistake instantly made them more confident using the app at all.

Contact Actions

Staff contact details shown as plain text — drivers would copy-paste numbers manually to call or message.

Quick-contact buttons (Call, WhatsApp) embedded directly in the staff modal, one tap from the timeline. This eliminated the copy-paste friction that was causing drivers to use the wrong channels and miss time-sensitive communication during pickups.

A timeline that gives drivers and dispatchers one shared truth.

The shipped concept is a timeline-centred mobile app where each job is a large card showing the job window, location map, and staff list with real-time status badges — all visible without a single tap.

Screen Deep-Dive

Three screens. Every design decision visible.

These are the three core screens of the product. Each one maps directly to a design decision made in response to a specific field finding.

Timeline View — Staff Check-in Modal

The whole roster, one screen

Tapping "Staffs in my car" opens this view — all assigned staff, their current check-in state, and large green Check-in buttons. Starting kilometer and Morning Call Time visible at the top so drivers confirm both before the day begins.

Decision

Two states side by side (Checked-in outline vs solid green Check-in) — drivers instantly see who still needs action without scanning the whole list.

Decision

Persistent "Staffs in my car (5)" pill stays visible even inside the modal — passenger count never leaves the driver's awareness.

Timeline Card View

The full day as ordered cards

Each job is a large card — location name, time window, inline map thumbnail, and per-staff Drop Off buttons. No tapping into a sub-screen for any of this. The entire route is readable in one scroll.

Decision

Map thumbnail inline on each card — drivers recognise the area visually before they read the address, saving 2–3 seconds per stop.

Trade-off

Cards are tall, limiting how many fit on screen — accepted this trade-off deliberately; better to read 2 jobs clearly than squint at 6 cramped ones.

Staff Detail Screen

Every key action for one staff member, one screen

Tapping a staff member opens their detail view — current status (Checked-In), upcoming job with location map, and two bottom actions: Check-Out Staff and Undo "Check-In." This screen was the most debated in design review.

Decision

"Coming Up" + "Drop Off" shown prominently above the job details — drivers know what action is coming next without reading anything.

Decision

Undo "Check-In" kept as a bottom action — not hidden in settings. Drivers said knowing it was there made them more confident tapping Check-in in the first place.

Problem found

Early version showed both Check-Out and Undo as equal-weight buttons — usability testing showed drivers mis-tapped Undo when they meant Check-Out. Final version makes Check-Out the clear primary CTA.

Challenges I Solved

The hard design problems.

Challenge
Problem
Solution
Designing for a user who can't look at their phone for more than 3 seconds

Drivers are in a moving vehicle, managing passengers, watching traffic. A complex UI isn't just bad UX — it's a safety risk.

Every core action reduced to one tap. Every status readable in one glance. Large touch targets throughout. Countdowns visible at card level — no drilling in required. The design's success criterion was: does it work at a red light?

Two users with opposing information needs

Drivers needed simplicity and speed. Dispatchers needed detailed, accurate status visibility. A single product had to serve both without compromising either.

Designed the driver-facing timeline as the primary interface, with status badges that automatically surface the data dispatchers need as a byproduct — no separate dispatcher dashboard required for core monitoring.

Making mistake recovery a feature, not an exception

Check-in errors were treated as edge cases — fixed by calling dispatch. This created anxiety and slowed drivers down, since they were afraid to tap the wrong action.

Elevated Undo to a first-class feature visible immediately after every action. Designed the system to treat errors as expected, not exceptional — which paradoxically reduced errors by removing fear of tapping.

Replacing a multi-channel habit with a single interface

Drivers had been managing routes across calls, WhatsApp, and a basic job list for months. Getting them to trust one app meant the app had to visibly do more than all three channels combined.

Embedded Call and WhatsApp quick-contact directly in the staff modal — the app didn't replace other channels by removing them, it replaced them by making them faster inside the timeline. Drivers adopted it because it was strictly better, not because they were forced to switch.

Drivers went from juggling 3 apps to tapping one timeline.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

Context of use changes everything

Designing for a moving vehicle isn't just "make it mobile-friendly." It requires a complete rethink of interaction model, touch target size, information density, and what "good UX" means when a user has 3 seconds.

Undo is underrated as a design principle

Recoverability isn't a nice-to-have — it's what gives users the confidence to act at all. When drivers knew they could undo, they stopped double-checking everything and started trusting the interface.

A shared source of truth is itself a product feature

The most impactful thing the timeline did wasn't the countdown or the Undo button — it was giving drivers and dispatchers the same view of reality. Coordination calls dropped because both sides finally agreed on what was happening.

Users Emotional Architecture

Designing for feelings, not just functions.

Most workforce apps are designed around operational efficiency. This one was designed around emotional outcomes — because for a maid, the feeling of financial control isn’t a nice-to-have, it’s the product.

Primary

The primary emotional goal. Maids should feel relieved — not stressed — when they open the app. Every data point visible reduces one anxiety. Hours logged: relief. Payment confirmed: relief. Booking accepted: relief.

Empathy Map

What maids say, think, feel, and actually do.

Standard user interviews tell you what people say. Empathy mapping tells you where the gaps are between what they say and what they actually feel and do. Those gaps are where the real design opportunities live.

Says

“I just want a simple way to track my hours and see what I’ve earned.” She asks for simplicity but the underlying need is financial transparency — two different design targets.

Thinks

“Am I earning the right amount? I can’t tell.” She constantly worries about payment accuracy but doesn’t feel empowered to question it — because she has no data to back her up.

Feels

Anxious and frustrated by lack of transparency. Relieved when she can finally see a clear view of her earnings. The emotional gap between these two states is the design brief.

Does

Records hours in notebooks or basic phone notes. Checks with supervisors to confirm payments. These workarounds signal that the current system has failed her — and they’re the behaviours we needed to replace.

The Real Problem

Conceptual design project. This case study documents a fully designed, prototype-level product. While not yet shipped, it was built with real field research and a complete design system — ready for development handoff.

Nobody knew if the safety talk actually worked.

On construction sites, toolbox talks happen every morning. A supervisor reads from a sheet, workers stand and listen — or don’t. There’s no way to measure whether the critical safety information was actually communicated, retained, or even fully covered.

Supervisors often believed they’d covered all points when they hadn’t. Workers missed key messages. Management had no visibility into talk quality or completeness. And if something went wrong on site, there was no verifiable record of what safety guidance was — or wasn’t — delivered.

“There’s currently no reliable way to measure the quality and completeness of toolbox talks. This creates communication gaps that can directly affect on-site safety and compliance.”

Problem definition, field observation sessions
5–10 — Minutes, the typical toolbox talk window

Short enough that every minute matters. Long enough that distracted workers consistently miss key points. The design had to work inside this constraint, not around it.

0 — Existing tools that measure talk completeness

No competing product addressed this. Supervisors used printed sheets, notebooks, or memory. The market gap was real — and unaddressed.

3 — Stakeholder groups with misaligned needs

Supervisors (conducting talks), workers (receiving them), and management (verifying compliance) — each needing something different from the same session.

The Design Journey

From transcript tool to real-time guidance system.

I began by observing real toolbox talks in the field — documenting how they flow, where supervisors lose track, and where workers disengage. That observation changed everything about the design direction.

01
First sketches focused on transcripts

My initial direction captured what was said — full transcription, keyword matching, post-talk review. But that felt like documentation, not guidance. Supervisors saw it as a surveillance tool, not a support tool. I needed to shift the framing entirely.

02

Rather than committing to the first idea, I prototyped three fundamentally different flows and tested each with real field workers before deciding.

Tested — not chosen
One long instruction screen

A single screen explaining the entire process upfront, then capture. Workers skipped reading it entirely, which caused mistakes and repeated failed attempts.

Tested — not chosen
Photo-booth style capture

Multiple simultaneous angle captures, like a photo booth strip. Accurate results, but needed extra physical space and time — not practical for a busy site.

Chosen direction
Step-by-step guided flow with live feedback

Breaking registration into small guided steps with real-time visual cues. This finally felt right — low cognitive load, high accuracy, no instructions to read.

Version 53 — Light Theme
Instruction-forward, progress-led

Step label and percentage shown upfront. Instruction text sits above the capture frame so it's read before the camera engages, not during. A full-width "Take Photo" CTA keeps the action explicit rather than automatic.

Decision

Progress bar placed above the frame, not below — workers see how far along they are before they even position their face

Version 51 — Camera Theme
Native camera mental model

Explored a darker, viewfinder-style theme with corner brackets and a circular shutter button — mimicking the native iOS camera app that every user already knows how to use intuitively.

Trade-off

Felt more familiar, but the darker UI reduced visibility of the progress indicator — clarity took priority, so the lighter theme was carried forward

Design Journey

Turning the winning direction into a real flow.

Once the step-by-step approach won, these were the specific decisions that shaped the final experience.

Decision
Before
After
Face Alignment Guidance

Text instructions like "Position your face within the frame" with no visual aid.

A soft face-outline overlay with directional arrows and a subtle green highlight when aligned correctly. Users could feel correct positioning without reading anything.

Feedback Tone

Technical error states: "Face detection failed" / "Alignment error code 04".

Plain-English, conversational prompts: "Good lighting!" or "Face not centered." Avoided sudden warnings or cold technical language entirely — the tone needed to feel like guidance, not judgment.

Progress Visibility

No progress indication — users didn't know how many steps remained or if they were close to done.

Added a clear progress indicator (0% → 60% → 80% → 100%) across the straight, slight-right, and slight-left captures. This single addition reduced abandonment mid-flow significantly in testing.

Flexibility to Exit

Registration was mandatory and blocking — users couldn't proceed into the app without completing it immediately.

Added a "Do Later" option during onboarding, with a clear path to complete registration later from the profile screen. Removing forced urgency actually increased voluntary completion rates.

Visual Noise

Branded UI with colour accents, icons, and decorative elements around the capture frame.

Stripped to a neutral palette and minimalist layout, keeping all visual attention on the user's face and the live feedback. Every element not essential to the task was removed.

Challenges I Solved

The hard design problems.

Designing for a moment people are naturally suspicious of
Problem

Biometric capture inherently feels invasive. Workers worried about surveillance, data misuse, or simply "looking bad" on camera in front of coworkers.

Solution

Replaced clinical language with conversational guidance, added a short plain-English privacy note instead of legal text, and made every interaction feel like the app was assisting rather than judging.

Workers skipping instructions entirely
Problem

The first prototype relied on a single instruction screen. Almost nobody read it fully, leading directly to failed scans and frustration.

Solution

Moved all guidance into the live capture screen itself — visual overlays and short contextual prompts replaced the need to read anything upfront.

Balancing accuracy with practicality on a job site
Problem

The photo-booth style approach captured more angles accurately, but required physical space and time that didn't fit real construction site conditions.

Solution

Narrowed capture to three essential angles — straight, slight right, slight left — enough for reliable recognition without demanding a studio-like setup.

Designing for failure without it feeling like failure
Problem

Early error states used technical, alarming language that made users feel like they'd done something wrong, increasing anxiety around retries.

Solution

Rewrote every error and incomplete state in calm, conversational language — "Face not centered" instead of "Detection failed" — and made retrying feel like a normal part of the flow, not a penalty.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

Trust is a design outcome, not a feature

Trust isn't something you bolt on with a privacy policy link. It's built pixel by pixel, through tone, pacing, and how a system responds when someone makes a mistake.

Micro-decisions shape perception

The difference between "Detection failed" and "Face not centered" seems small. In practice, it's the difference between a user feeling judged and a user feeling guided.

Accessibility deserves earlier thought

Biometric systems need real consideration for users with facial coverings or challenging lighting conditions — this is a design responsibility, not an edge case to defer.

The Human Impact

What changed wasn't just completion rate — it was the emotion behind it.

In testing, users smiled when they saw live feedback for the first time. The biometric moment shifted from tense to cooperative.
