The Real Problem

Conceptual design project. This case study documents a fully designed, prototype-level product. While not yet shipped, it was built with real field research and a complete design system — ready for development handoff.

Nobody knew if the safety talk actually worked.

On construction sites, toolbox talks happen every morning. A supervisor reads from a sheet, workers stand and listen — or don’t. There’s no way to measure whether the critical safety information was actually communicated, retained, or even fully covered.

Supervisors often believed they’d covered all points when they hadn’t. Workers missed key messages. Management had no visibility into talk quality or completeness. And if something went wrong on site, there was no verifiable record of what safety guidance was — or wasn’t — delivered.

“There’s currently no reliable way to measure the quality and completeness of toolbox talks. This creates communication gaps that can directly affect on-site safety and compliance.”

Problem definition, field observation sessions
5–10 — Minutes, the typical toolbox talk window

Short enough that every minute matters. Long enough that distracted workers consistently miss key points. The design had to work inside this constraint, not around it.

0 — Existing tools that measure talk completeness

No competing product addressed this. Supervisors used printed sheets, notebooks, or memory. The market gap was real — and unaddressed.

3 — Stakeholder groups with misaligned needs

Supervisors (conducting talks), workers (receiving them), and management (verifying compliance) — each needing something different from the same session.

The Design Journey

From transcript tool to real-time guidance system.

I began by observing real toolbox talks in the field — documenting how they flow, where supervisors lose track, and where workers disengage. That observation changed everything about the design direction.

01
First sketches focused on transcripts

My initial direction captured what was said — full transcription, keyword matching, post-talk review. But that felt like documentation, not guidance. Supervisors saw it as a surveillance tool, not a support tool. I needed to shift the framing entirely.

02
Visual meters changed everything

I explored coverage bars and percentage indicators that filled as the AI recognised each checklist point being mentioned. This felt like real-time coaching rather than recording. The supervisor could glance at the screen and immediately know where they stood — without breaking the flow of the talk.

03
Tested multiple layout approaches

Progress bars, point-based lists, and colour-coded summaries were all prototyped and tested. What stood out most: visual immediacy — a design that clearly showed “You’ve covered 80% of the talk; 2 points left.” Users responded to specificity, not vagueness.

04
Tone was as important as information

Early prototypes felt judgmental — red indicators for missed points triggered defensiveness in supervisors. The final design reframed every negative state as an opportunity rather than a failure. “2 points remaining” instead of “2 points missed.” The system had to feel supportive, not evaluative.

05
Attendance was the missing piece

Field observation revealed that attendance recording was as problematic as talk quality. Supervisors were manually signing sheets — slow, inaccurate, and unfalsifiable. Adding face recognition attendance capture to the same flow solved both problems with one session.

Visual Language

A three-colour system that tells supervisors exactly where they stand.

The entire feedback system is built on three states. Supervisors learn them in the first session and never have to think about them again — the colour does the communication work so the supervisor can focus on the talk.

Fully covered — point well explained
Partially mentioned — needs more depth
Not yet covered — still remaining
Before the Talk
Structured checklist ready to go

Supervisor selects the toolbox topic — a predefined checklist of safety points appears. All points start as uncovered. The “Start & Speak” button is the only action needed. No setup, no configuration.

During the Talk — Live
AI listens and updates in real time

As the supervisor speaks, covered points turn green, partial mentions yellow, uncovered points stay red. A live progress bar and percentage update with every sentence. The supervisor sees where they are without stopping the talk.

After the Talk
Summary + attendance in one tap

AI generates a session summary — overall completion percentage, missed points, depth score per topic. Supervisor takes a group photo; face recognition logs attendance automatically. Management gets a complete, verified record.

Screen Deep-Dive

Before and during. Two states that tell the whole story.

The most important design decision in this product was the transition between these two states — from static checklist to live AI-annotated view. That transition is where supervisors feel the shift from documentation to real-time guidance.

Before the Talk
Checklist as briefing — not a form to fill

The supervisor sees exactly what needs to be covered before they start speaking. No cognitive load — the list is the script. “View Full Content” gives access to the detailed OSHA guidance without cluttering the primary flow.

Decision“Start & Speak” as a single large green CTA — zero ambiguity about what the supervisor does next, and the colour primes them for the green = covered feedback system they’re about to use
Decision“View Full Content” is secondary — supervisors don’t need to read everything; they need to cover key points. Full content is there for the detail-oriented, not as a required step
During the Talk — Live AI
Real-time coaching at a glance

65% progress bar shows completion at a glance without breaking the talk flow. Green-highlighted points confirm what’s been covered. Uncovered points remain visible — the supervisor can see exactly what’s left without counting or tracking manually.

DecisionLive timer (00:05:32) at the bottom — supervisors know how long the talk has run without checking a watch, helping them pace the remaining points within the session window
DecisionDone button in red — deliberately different from the green Start button. Red here means “end session,” not “error.” Testing showed this distinction was immediately understood without any label explanation needed

Final Solution

One interface. Three user groups. Zero paperwork.

The AI Toolbox Talk Analyzer brings together content analysis and attendance verification in a single, guided flow — giving supervisors real-time coaching, workers more consistent briefings, and management a complete verifiable record.

Challenges I Solved

The hard design problems.

01
Designing AI feedback that coaches rather than judges
The problem

Early prototypes with red “missed” indicators triggered defensiveness in supervisors. They felt audited, not helped — and became reluctant to use the tool at all.

My solution

Reframed every negative state as forward-looking. “2 points remaining” instead of “2 points missed.” Red stayed as a colour but the language shifted entirely to what’s next, not what failed. Testing showed this single change turned resistance into engagement.

02
Keeping the supervisor’s attention on workers, not the screen
The problem

An early version updated the progress percentage every few seconds. Supervisors started watching the screen mid-talk instead of addressing their workers — defeating the purpose of the tool entirely.

My solution

Smoothed the update frequency so progress felt like a background indicator rather than a live score. Point highlights updated clearly but without jerky animations that drew the eye. The screen became a peripheral reference, not a primary focus.

03
Building a product for three users with conflicting success metrics
The problem

Supervisors wanted to feel supported. Workers wanted shorter, clearer briefings. Management wanted verified compliance data. All three needed to be served from one session — and none of their needs could be prioritised at another’s expense.

My solution

Designed the supervisor experience as the primary interface (since they drive the session), with the management data layer as a byproduct of that flow — not a separate process. Workers benefited automatically when supervisors improved their talks based on the real-time feedback.

04
Making NLP-powered AI feel accurate and trustworthy on a job site
The problem

Construction site environments are noisy. Supervisors speak in varied accents and informal language. AI misidentification of covered points would destroy trust in the system immediately.

My solution

Designed the UI to show partial coverage (yellow) rather than forcing binary covered/not-covered states. This made the AI feel honest about uncertainty rather than falsely confident. Supervisors could also manually mark a point as covered — keeping them in control even when AI confidence was lower.

The Human Impact

Structure and accountability for an often-overlooked daily practice.

Though still conceptual, this tool addresses a real gap in construction site safety culture — one where the most important daily safety practice leaves no record, no feedback, and no improvement loop.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

AI is most powerful when it stays invisible

The best version of this product is one where supervisors don’t think about the AI at all — they just notice that their talks are getting better. The moment the AI becomes the thing users are interacting with instead of the tool helping them interact with their workers, it has failed its design brief.

Partial states are more honest than binary ones

Forcing everything into covered/not-covered made the AI feel overconfident and untrustworthy. Adding “partially mentioned” (yellow) made the system feel accurate and humble — and paradoxically increased user trust in the green and red states too.

Safety tools have a higher bar for tone than any other product type

If a productivity app feels judgmental, users close it. If a safety compliance tool feels judgmental, it gets actively avoided — and the safety gap it was meant to close gets worse. The stakes of getting tone wrong in safety design are not just UX problems, they’re real-world consequences.
