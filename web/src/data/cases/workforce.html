The Real Problem

Attendance ran on queues, paper and Excel.

Construction sites were relying on punching machines, paper attendance registers, Excel spreadsheets and separate systems to track employee attendance. Workers often had to wait in long queues to punch in and out, while some tried to manipulate punch times to gain additional minutes or hours.

After punching, HR or the site document controller would retrieve the data, print it, send it to a supervisor for review and confirmation, and then forward it to accounts for payroll processing. The process was slow, manual and difficult to manage across a large field workforce.

Cost leakage

Manipulated punch times and manual corrections turned into paid hours nobody could verify.

Administrative pressure

HR and supervisors spent significant time reviewing, printing and correcting attendance data.

No worker visibility

Workers had limited access to their own records, so pay questions became HR queues too.

Design Journey — Decision Log

Six decisions that shaped the product.

Why replace punching machines?

Initial: continue relying on traditional punching-machine attendance.

Moved attendance completely to mobile check-in/out using face verification. The old process created queues and opportunities for punch-time manipulation; mobile made it faster while giving the system context about who, where and when.

Why combine face with location?

Initial: a simple digital check-in.

Combined AI face verification with GPS/geofencing. Face verification connects attendance to the correct worker; geofencing verifies they are within the assigned project location.

What happens when AI fails?

Initial: depend completely on automated face recognition.

Added a manual attendance fallback. If the face is not recognised, the worker can still record attendance manually and the record goes to their team leader for verification — a recognition failure never becomes lost working time.

How much control for supervisors?

Initial: make attendance as automated as possible.

Gave supervisors controlled editing and approval permissions. They can modify work timings, approve manual attendance, manage leave, change work status, and manage awards, overtime hours, fines and ratings.

How should overtime move?

Initial: treat overtime as another manual payroll input.

Calculated overtime automatically from working-time rules, then routed it for approval: automatic calculation → supervisor → HR → payroll, with supervisors able to modify timings when required.

Should workers see their own pay?

Initial: keep attendance and salary inside HR/payroll processes.

Gave workers direct visibility into attendance, timesheets and monthly salary. This connects the work they record with the pay they receive and reduces dependency on HR for basic information.

Unique Contribution

Manual attendance recovery — when the AI is wrong.

Automation should make attendance easier, but it should not become a single point of failure. If the AI cannot recognise a worker, the worker can still capture their attendance manually instead of losing the actual working time — and the record is then verified by their supervisor.

Attendance continuity

A recognition failure does not automatically mean lost attendance.

Supervisor accountability

Manual records require verification before they count.

Low-friction recovery

Workers get an immediate alternative instead of being blocked by an AI error.

Challenges I Solved

The hard design problems.

01
Preventing attendance fraud without complicating check-in
The problem

Attendance needed to be accountable without creating another slow process for workers.

My solution

Single-photo face verification combined with GPS/geofencing and assigned shift timings. The worker only needs a simple selfie, while the system checks the person, project location and working-time context.

02
Handling AI face-recognition failures without losing attendance
The problem

AI recognition can fail, and blocking a worker from recording genuine attendance would create another unfairness.

My solution

A manual attendance fallback. The worker still captures attendance through a selfie without AI recognition, but the record requires verification from their designated team leader.

03
Designing for workers from different language backgrounds
The problem

Workers come from different countries and may not be comfortable typing or explaining requests in English.

My solution

Voice reasoning for leave and other applicable requests — workers speak in their own language and AI transcribes the reason into English. For attendance, the interaction is reduced to an action that does not depend on language.

04
Giving supervisors control without bringing back the manual process
The problem

Full automation would remove necessary human judgement, but too much manual control could recreate the old workflow.

My solution

Controlled permissions around specific decisions: approve leave, approve manual attendance, award overtime hours, issue fines, rate workers, edit work timings and change work status.

The Human Impact

No more queues, and no more guessing about pay.

Workers no longer had to queue at punching machines and gained direct visibility into their attendance and salary. Supervisors spent less time on manual attendance work, HR had fewer manual attendance and payroll tasks, disputes were reduced, and payroll preparation became faster.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

AI needs a human fallback

Automation should not become a single point of failure. The face-recognition fallback taught me that a good AI experience also needs a clear recovery path when the system cannot confidently complete the task.

Constraints can make UX better

Combining face verification, location and working-time restrictions sounds restrictive, but together they created a simpler and more accountable attendance experience. The worker gets a quick interaction while the system handles the complexity behind it.

Design for diversity by reducing language dependency

Designing for a multilingual workforce made me think beyond translation. Voice input and simple, action-based interactions reduce the amount of language users need to understand or type in the first place.
