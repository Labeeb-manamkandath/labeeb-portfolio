The Real Problem

Maids were losing income they had already earned.

Field maids working through YellowZap had no unified way to track their hours, monitor their earnings, or manage their bookings. Work was logged in notebooks. Earnings were disputed. Reviews were scattered across platforms where no one could act on them.

The consequences were financial: missed logging meant missed pay. The consequences were emotional: maids felt uncertain and unseen. The design challenge was to give them a tool that felt personal to them — not just a job management system imposed from above.

“She often asks for a simple way to track her working hours and earnings. She complains about forgetting to record her time, which results in missed pay.”

Empathy map finding, field research sessions
30+ — In-depth individual interviews

Conducted during actual workdays — not in an office. Observed real work-tracking behaviour in context, not self-reported recall.

3 — Core pain points identified

Inconsistent tracking (manual logs causing errors), unclear earnings (no visibility into monthly progress), and scattered feedback (reviews lost across apps).

Users Emotional Architecture

Designing for feelings, not just functions.

Most workforce apps are designed around operational efficiency. This one was designed around emotional outcomes — because for a maid, the feeling of financial control isn’t a nice-to-have, it’s the product.

Primary
Relief

The primary emotional goal. Maids should feel relieved — not stressed — when they open the app. Every data point visible reduces one anxiety. Hours logged: relief. Payment confirmed: relief. Booking accepted: relief.

Secondary
Pride

The secondary emotional outcome. The performance dashboard, rating display, and incentive tracker were all designed to make maids feel proud of their work — not just informed about it. Seeing 4.3 stars next to your name does something numbers alone can’t.

Deepest need
Control

The deepest need. Maids felt powerless over their earnings and schedule. Every design decision — from the salary calculator to the Accept/Decline job flow — was made to give them genuine agency, not the illusion of it.

Empathy Map

What maids say, think, feel, and actually do.

Standard user interviews tell you what people say. Empathy mapping tells you where the gaps are between what they say and what they actually feel and do. Those gaps are where the real design opportunities live.

Says

“I just want a simple way to track my hours and see what I’ve earned.” She asks for simplicity but the underlying need is financial transparency — two different design targets.

Thinks

“Am I earning the right amount? I can’t tell.” She constantly worries about payment accuracy but doesn’t feel empowered to question it — because she has no data to back her up.

Feels

Anxious and frustrated by lack of transparency. Relieved when she can finally see a clear view of her earnings. The emotional gap between these two states is the design brief.

Does

Records hours in notebooks or basic phone notes. Checks with supervisors to confirm payments. These workarounds signal that the current system has failed her — and they’re the behaviours we needed to replace.

Design Journey

The decisions that turned anxiety into control.

Each pivot came from a specific research finding — not a preference, not a trend.

Decision
Before
After
Earnings Visibility

A simple total earnings figure shown at the end of the month — clean, minimal.

A real-time salary calculator showing current earnings, projected monthly total, and hours-to-incentive target. Research showed that monthly figures produced anxiety (too far away, too abstract). Daily/weekly visibility gave maids the sense of progress that motivated more consistent work.

Booking Accept / Decline

Job details buried inside a card — tap to expand, then find the accept button.

A dedicated Booking Details screen with full job info (service type, duration, cleaners, payment, location, deadline) and two prominent CTAs — Decline (left, red) and Accept Job (right, yellow). Urgency indicator “Accept job by 3:30 PM” shown prominently. Testing showed that buried actions caused decision paralysis and job misses.

Dashboard Information Hierarchy

A generic home screen with upcoming jobs and a notification feed — operational, not personal.

A personalised dashboard (Sheryl — Dashboard) showing Total Hours, Jobs Done, and Rating as the three headline metrics — then Work Days vs Absent Days, today’s hours, and a Performance Trend tracker. The personal name and performance framing made maids feel ownership over their own data, not just visibility into company records.

Missed Job Communication

Missed jobs simply disappeared from the bookings list — no acknowledgement, no context.

A distinct “You missed this job” card state with an emoji, pink background, and timestamp. This decision was uncomfortable to include — nobody wants to show failure — but testing revealed that transparent tracking of misses was what maids most needed to improve. Seeing missed jobs helped them understand patterns and make better decisions about schedule management.

Performance Incentives

Incentive thresholds communicated via supervisor messaging — no visibility in the app itself.

A visible target line on the Performance Trend chart showing hours required for the next incentive tier. “73.5 Hours — Required for incentives” shown as a persistent callout. Making the target visible turned an abstract manager goal into a personal achievement milestone that maids actually worked toward.

Unique Contribution

The Salary Calculator — financial clarity as a product feature.

Most workforce apps show what happened. This feature showed what was about to happen — giving maids the ability to project their earnings and plan their schedule around financial goals, not just accept whatever work came in.

Unique Feature
The Salary Calculator

Before this feature, maids had no way to answer the question “How much will I earn this month?” Now they could. Enter the hours worked and planned, and the calculator shows projected earnings, current standing against target, and hours needed for the next incentive tier — all in real time.

Real-time projectionCurrent earnings + planned hours = projected monthly total — visible without any calculation effort from the maid
Incentive target trackerShows exactly how many more hours are needed to reach the next earnings tier — turns a vague goal into a concrete number
Financial empowermentThe first time many maids had a tool that treated them as financial decision-makers, not just job recipients — adoption was immediate
Screen Deep-Dive

Three screens. Every design decision has a research reason.

Dashboard
Personal performance, not ops reporting

Using the maid’s name (“Sheryl — Dashboard”) makes this feel like her data, not the company’s data about her. Three headline metrics — hours, jobs, rating — show the things maids care about most. Absent Days in pink creates appropriate visibility without shame.

DecisionPerformance Trend chart shows target line at 153 hours — making the incentive goal a visible daily reference, not an abstract supervisor-communicated number
DecisionToday’s work shown as a yellow timeline bar — maids see their current day progress at a glance without navigating to a calendar
Bookings Feed
Three states, one scan

Confirmed bookings (green left border + gold header), missed jobs (pink background + emoji), and pending bookings each have distinct visual signatures. Maids understand their full booking status without reading a single word — colour and shape do the work.

Decision“You missed this job” shown in the feed rather than hidden — transparent failure tracking was what maids most needed to improve consistency
ChallengeShowing missed jobs felt uncomfortable in design review — stakeholders worried it would frustrate maids. Testing proved the opposite: maids wanted accountability, not protection from it
Booking Details Screen
Everything needed to make a confident decision — one screen, no back-and-forth

The booking details screen was designed around one question: what does a maid need to know before she says yes or no to a job? The answer was: service type, duration, team size, payment amount, exact location, and how long she has to decide.

DecisionPayment shown as its own green card (AED 246 — Cash on job site) — financial clarity is the #1 factor in job acceptance decisions, so it gets visual prominence equal to date and time
Decision“Free slots available” indicator next to the time — reduces the anxiety of accepting a job that might conflict with something already scheduled
ProblemEarly versions had Accept and Decline as equal-sized buttons — testing showed maids accidentally tapped Decline when they meant to scroll. Final version makes Accept Job the full-width primary CTA with Decline clearly secondary

Challenges I Solved

The hard design problems.

01
Designing for users with low digital literacy in a high-stakes context
The problem

Many maids were not regular smartphone app users. Complex UI patterns caused confusion and abandonment — but the app needed to surface dense information (earnings, bookings, performance) without overwhelming users.

My solution

Visual language over text at every opportunity. Colour-coded booking states, progress bars for performance, a dashboard that reads at a glance without scrolling. Reduced reading load to near zero for all core tasks.

02
Making earnings visible without making failures feel punishing
The problem

Showing missed jobs and absent days risked making the app feel like surveillance — a tool for management to monitor maids rather than a tool that served them.

My solution

Framed all negative states in the context of improvement, not judgment. Absent Days in pink (not red). Missed jobs shown with emoji and neutral timestamp, not alarm language. The tone throughout is “here’s your data” not “here’s what you did wrong.”

03
Building trust with a user group that had been let down by digital tools before
The problem

Maids had used basic apps and notebooks that failed them — hours untracked, earnings disputed, reviews lost. Their starting point was scepticism, not openness.

My solution

The salary calculator became the trust-building feature — because it gave maids something no previous tool had: the ability to verify their own earnings independently. When a maid could calculate her pay herself and see it match what she received, the app earned her trust in a way no onboarding flow could.

04
Designing one app that served maids and satisfied business stakeholders
The problem

YellowZap needed operational data — job completion rates, attendance, performance trends. Maids needed personal empowerment. These two needs can produce conflicting designs if not handled carefully.

My solution

Designed the maid experience first, then verified that the same data satisfied operational needs as a byproduct. When a maid tracks her own hours for her own financial clarity, she’s simultaneously providing the data the business needs. The maid’s benefit was the primary design goal — the business value was the result.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

Empathy mapping changes what you design, not just how you feel about it

Discovering the gap between what maids said (“I want simple tracking”) and what they felt (“I’m anxious about whether I’m being paid correctly”) changed the entire product direction. The salary calculator didn’t come from a feature request — it came from the empathy map.

Transparency is kinder than protection

The instinct in design is often to hide negative information — missed jobs, absent days — to protect users’ feelings. This project taught me the opposite: users who see their real data make better decisions and feel more respected. Hiding it is patronising, not kind.

Design thinking scales from personal tools to enterprise revenue

The same empathy that produced the salary calculator produced the dynamic profile and visual flyer features. Good product thinking doesn’t stop at the core use case — it finds adjacent opportunities that serve the user and the business simultaneously.
