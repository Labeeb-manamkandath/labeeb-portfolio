The Real Problem

Nobody knew who was actually in the vehicle.

On the surface, the ask was simple: "show routes in an app." But the real problem ran deeper. Drivers didn't know who was actually in the vehicle, when the next job started, or how long they had before they needed to leave. Dispatchers were equally blind.

The result was a constant loop of phone calls, WhatsApp messages, missed pickups, and idle time. Drivers were managing routes across three different channels simultaneously — while driving. That's not a productivity problem. That's a safety problem.

"The deeper problem was uncertainty and friction — drivers didn't know who was actually in the vehicle, when the next job starts, or how long until they must leave."

Problem framing from field observation sessions
3
Channels drivers monitored simultaneously

Phone calls, WhatsApp groups, and a basic job list app — none of them showing a shared, real-time view of who's in the car.

2
Core user types with conflicting needs

Drivers needed glanceable, one-tap simplicity. Dispatchers needed accurate status visibility and route verification. One product had to serve both.

4
Critical friction points identified

Missed pickups, wrong staff check-ins, no buffer time visibility, and no way to undo a mistake without calling dispatch.

User Flows & Information Architecture

Three principles for an interface used while driving.

Principle 01
Glanceability

Show the whole day in one view. A driver pulling up to a stop needs to understand their next three hours in under 2 seconds — before they open the car door.

Principle 02
Low friction

Large touch targets and one-tap actions only. No multi-step confirmations, no navigation to a second screen to complete a core task. If it takes more than one tap, it's too slow.

Principle 03
Recoverability

Mistakes happen on the road. Every action — check-in, drop-off, pick-up — must be undoable in one tap without calling dispatch, filling a form, or losing any data.

Visual Language

A colour system drivers read without thinking.

Every screen element had to be interpretable at a glance. I built a four-state badge system that drivers learn in the first 60 seconds and never have to re-learn.

Checked-in — staff is in the vehicle
Pending — not yet confirmed
Not in car — absent or unassigned
Declined — staff rejected the job

Information Architecture

A list view showing jobs sorted by time, similar to a calendar app. Clean, familiar, standard.

A timeline-centred view where each job is a large card with location map, time window, staff list, and two primary CTA buttons visible without scrolling. Field testing showed that list views forced drivers to mentally reconstruct the day — the timeline made the whole day instantly readable as a sequence.

Staff Visibility

Staff status shown only inside individual job cards — tap the job to see who's in the vehicle.

A persistent "Staffs in my car (N)" button always visible on the main timeline, opening a roster modal with Check-in, Undo, Drop-off, and Pick-up actions. Research showed the most common driver anxiety was not knowing their current passenger count — making it persistent removed that anxiety entirely.

Check-in Action

Inline check-in within the job card, small tap target, adjacent to other buttons.

Large full-width green Check-in button as the primary CTA. Red Undo button immediately accessible. Button size was driven by the use context — drivers are often in motion, glancing at the screen for under 3 seconds. Small targets caused mis-taps that required calling dispatch to correct.

Time Buffer Visibility

No time buffer display — drivers could see job start times but had to mentally calculate travel time themselves.

Live countdown between jobs: "30 mins to start next job." This single element reduced late departures significantly in testing — drivers stopped relying on memory and gut instinct for departure timing and trusted the visible countdown instead.

Mistake Recovery

Wrong check-ins required contacting dispatch to manually correct — treated as an exception, not part of the main flow.

One-tap Undo built into every action as a first-class feature, not an afterthought. Drivers in testing described the Undo button as "the most important thing in the app" — knowing they could correct a mistake instantly made them more confident using the app at all.

Contact Actions

Staff contact details shown as plain text — drivers would copy-paste numbers manually to call or message.

Quick-contact buttons (Call, WhatsApp) embedded directly in the staff modal, one tap from the timeline. This eliminated the copy-paste friction that was causing drivers to use the wrong channels and miss time-sensitive communication during pickups.

A timeline that gives drivers and dispatchers one shared truth.

The shipped concept is a timeline-centred mobile app where each job is a large card showing the job window, location map, and staff list with real-time status badges — all visible without a single tap.

Screen Deep-Dive

Three screens. Every design decision visible.

These are the three core screens of the product. Each one maps directly to a design decision made in response to a specific field finding.

Timeline View — Staff Check-in Modal

The whole roster, one screen

Tapping "Staffs in my car" opens this view — all assigned staff, their current check-in state, and large green Check-in buttons. Starting kilometer and Morning Call Time visible at the top so drivers confirm both before the day begins.

Decision

Two states side by side (Checked-in outline vs solid green Check-in) — drivers instantly see who still needs action without scanning the whole list.

Decision

Persistent "Staffs in my car (5)" pill stays visible even inside the modal — passenger count never leaves the driver's awareness.

Timeline Card View

The full day as ordered cards

Each job is a large card — location name, time window, inline map thumbnail, and per-staff Drop Off buttons. No tapping into a sub-screen for any of this. The entire route is readable in one scroll.

Decision

Map thumbnail inline on each card — drivers recognise the area visually before they read the address, saving 2–3 seconds per stop.

Trade-off

Cards are tall, limiting how many fit on screen — accepted this trade-off deliberately; better to read 2 jobs clearly than squint at 6 cramped ones.

Staff Detail Screen

Every key action for one staff member, one screen

Tapping a staff member opens their detail view — current status (Checked-In), upcoming job with location map, and two bottom actions: Check-Out Staff and Undo "Check-In." This screen was the most debated in design review.

Decision

"Coming Up" + "Drop Off" shown prominently above the job details — drivers know what action is coming next without reading anything.

Decision

Undo "Check-In" kept as a bottom action — not hidden in settings. Drivers said knowing it was there made them more confident tapping Check-in in the first place.

Problem found

Early version showed both Check-Out and Undo as equal-weight buttons — usability testing showed drivers mis-tapped Undo when they meant Check-Out. Final version makes Check-Out the clear primary CTA.

Challenges I Solved

The hard design problems.

Challenge
Problem
Solution
Designing for a user who can't look at their phone for more than 3 seconds

Drivers are in a moving vehicle, managing passengers, watching traffic. A complex UI isn't just bad UX — it's a safety risk.

Every core action reduced to one tap. Every status readable in one glance. Large touch targets throughout. Countdowns visible at card level — no drilling in required. The design's success criterion was: does it work at a red light?

Two users with opposing information needs

Drivers needed simplicity and speed. Dispatchers needed detailed, accurate status visibility. A single product had to serve both without compromising either.

Designed the driver-facing timeline as the primary interface, with status badges that automatically surface the data dispatchers need as a byproduct — no separate dispatcher dashboard required for core monitoring.

Making mistake recovery a feature, not an exception

Check-in errors were treated as edge cases — fixed by calling dispatch. This created anxiety and slowed drivers down, since they were afraid to tap the wrong action.

Elevated Undo to a first-class feature visible immediately after every action. Designed the system to treat errors as expected, not exceptional — which paradoxically reduced errors by removing fear of tapping.

Replacing a multi-channel habit with a single interface

Drivers had been managing routes across calls, WhatsApp, and a basic job list for months. Getting them to trust one app meant the app had to visibly do more than all three channels combined.

Embedded Call and WhatsApp quick-contact directly in the staff modal — the app didn't replace other channels by removing them, it replaced them by making them faster inside the timeline. Drivers adopted it because it was strictly better, not because they were forced to switch.

The Human Impact

Drivers went from juggling 3 apps to tapping one timeline.

The biggest shift wasn't operational — it was emotional. Drivers reported feeling calmer and more in control. Staff felt less anxious because check-in and drop-off states were visible and correctable.

Reflections

What this project taught me.

Context of use changes everything

Designing for a moving vehicle isn't just "make it mobile-friendly." It requires a complete rethink of interaction model, touch target size, information density, and what "good UX" means when a user has 3 seconds.

Undo is underrated as a design principle

Recoverability isn't a nice-to-have — it's what gives users the confidence to act at all. When drivers knew they could undo, they stopped double-checking everything and started trusting the interface.

A shared source of truth is itself a product feature

The most impactful thing the timeline did wasn't the countdown or the Undo button — it was giving drivers and dispatchers the same view of reality. Coordination calls dropped because both sides finally agreed on what was happening.
